Porter's value chain

Michael Porter's 1985 idea breaks a firm into the activities it performs. Competitive advantage lives in those activities, not in the firm as a whole, because a firm has no costs. Activities do. Porter sorted them into nine generic boxes.

The chain (tap a box)

Blue-green boxes are primary activities that touch the product on its way to the buyer. Gold boxes are support activities that run across all of them.

Two things people miss

1. The boxes are a starting point. Split an activity when its parts have different economics. "Operations" at an airline is not one activity: gate turnaround and engine overhaul share almost nothing.

2. The advantage often sits between the boxes. Porter calls these linkages: the cost of one activity depends on how another is done. More inbound inspection means less rework. More inventory means less expediting.

Why linkages make chains hard to copy

A rival can see any single activity: store layouts, shelf prices, job postings, patents. What it cannot easily see is which activity is subsidizing which.

Amazon's 2023 network redesign is the reading's example. No single activity got cheaper. The relationships among them changed, and cost per unit fell for the first time since 2018.

How to run a value chain analysis

The framework only describes. The analysis is five steps. Each step has a predictable way to fail when you skip its questions. Open each one.

Own one link

The opposite of Little Caesars is to take one activity and perform it so far beyond anyone else that the rest of the industry comes to you. Five examples, one per activity.

Inbound logistics

ASML: lithography

The only maker of EUV lithography systems, with an estimated 94 percent share of all lithography (JPMorgan estimate). 2025 sales of €32.7 billion, 52.8 percent gross margin, 34.6 percent operating margin, €38.8 billion backlog.

The mechanism: ASML is a systems integrator coordinating about 5,100 suppliers. In 2016 it bought 24.9 percent of Carl Zeiss SMT, the only optics house that can make EUV mirrors. A rival would have to rebuild a supplier network built over three decades.

Operations

TSMC: fabrication capacity

Owns no product designs. Took 72.5 percent of global foundry revenue in Q2 2026 (a record), with a 67.7 percent gross margin and 60.3 percent operating margin. Samsung had 5.9 percent.

The mechanism: A promise, not a technology. TSMC never competes with its customers. That let Nvidia, Apple, AMD and Qualcomm shed their fabs, which concentrated demand, which funds the node lead.

Operations (supplier)

YKK: zippers

About 40 percent of the world's zippers, more than 10 billion units in the year to March 2025. It smelts its own brass, makes its own alloys, dyes its tape, and builds its own fastening machinery.

The mechanism: It leases machines into customers' factories, so its fixed assets sit on the garment maker's floor. The zipper is about 1 percent of a garment's cost, yet its logo is often the only one on the product.

Caveat: YKK is private and publishes no margins. The reading says so out loud.

Outbound logistics

Amazon: parcels

In calendar 2025 it delivered 6.7 billion US parcels, passing USPS and becoming the largest carrier. In May 2026 it began selling the capability through Amazon Supply Chain Services.

The mechanism: An internal cost center, optimized until it became an advantage, then sold to the incumbents' own customers. FedEx and UPS shares each fell about 10 percent on the news.

Service

The Ritz-Carlton

Any employee may spend up to $2,000 on the spot to fix a guest problem. Revenue per available room beat the industry by more than 300 percent. Pre-tax ROI nearly doubled from 5.3 to 9.8 percent (1995 to 1998). First-year staff trained 250 to 310 hours. Turnover fell nine straight years.

Source is a 1999 Baldrige profile. Treat the figures as historical, not current.

Profit pools: where the money sits

If activities have different economics, the money piles up in some parts of any chain. A value chain map comes from your own ledger and shows where costs are. A profit pool map comes from industry filings and shows where everyone's profits are. The first is an efficiency tool. The second asks whether you stand in the right part of the chain at all.

Check yourself

Tap a question to reveal the answer. Then read the cheat sheet.

1. What does Porter say a firm actually has?
No costs. Activities have costs. A firm wins or loses in each activity separately.
2. Name the two kinds of linkage the reading uses as an example.
Inbound inspection reduces rework. Holding inventory reduces expediting. Also: designing a part to snap instead of bolt shortens assembly.
3. Why does Little Caesars' low price only work with high throughput per labor hour?
Made-ahead production delivers the throughput. Without it, a low price cannot cover labor. Remove any one of narrow menu, made-ahead production, or low price and the other two stop making sense.
4. Why is Little Caesars' volume a fragility rather than a strength?
It pairs low cost with low throughput, unlike Walmart. Fixed chain costs (distribution, national advertising, brand) are spread over fewer dollars.
5. What is a profit pool, and how does it differ from a value chain map?
A profit pool is the total profit earned at every point along an industry's chain. The value chain map uses your own ledger to show costs. The profit pool uses industry filings to show where everyone's profits sit. The first is an efficiency instrument; the second is a positioning instrument.
6. Why does the reading say the pattern of profit differs from revenue?
Profit concentrates in different places than revenue. In a car dealership, parts, service, finance and insurance are 22.8 percent of sales but 77.2 percent of gross profit.
7. What did Amazon's 2023 network redesign change, and what did it not change?
It regionalized the US into eight largely self-sufficient regions. No single activity got cheaper. The relationships among them changed, and cost per unit fell.
8. What is the "key distinction" the reading ends on?
A value chain analysis shows where the cost went out. It does not show who kept it. Ask both questions.

    One-card cheat sheet

    1. Value chain: a firm is a set of activities, each with a cost. Advantage lives in the activities and the links between them.
    2. Run it in five steps: split by economics, attach cost and assets, find cost drivers (ten of them), work the linkages, decide make vs. buy.
    3. Linkages: the hard-to-copy part. Competitors can see activities but not which ones subsidize which.
    4. Delete an activity is the most reliable cost cut, because matching a rival's performance invites a response.
    5. Own one link: be so good at one activity that the industry comes to you (ASML, TSMC, YKK, Amazon).
    6. Profit pool: map where industry profit sits, using filings. Revenue and profit concentrate in different places.
    7. Two questions: where did the cost go out, and who kept it?
