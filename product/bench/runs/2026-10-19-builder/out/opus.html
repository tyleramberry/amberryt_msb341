STRAT 401 · Reading, without the reading

A pizza that cost five dollars, and what it teaches about strategy

A firm doesn't have costs. Activities do. This page walks through the whole reading: Porter's value chain, how to run the analysis, the Little Caesars case and what it hides, firms that win by owning one activity, and profit pools (where the money actually sits).

Garden City, Michigan. A pepperoni pizza sits in a warming rack. Nobody has ordered it yet. It was made to a forecast. When a customer walks in, the sale takes about 40 seconds. Price: $5, from 2004 until January 2022.

The obvious explanation is cheap ingredients, and it's wrong. The $5 pizza came from about a dozen activities, each built to cost less than the same activity at Domino's or Pizza Hut. One of them, the delivery driver, was removed entirely.

The one-sentence takeawayA value chain analysis breaks a firm into the things it actually does, so you can ask three questions of each one: What does it cost us? What drives that cost? Would a buyer notice if we did it differently?
Part 1 · The framework

What Porter actually proposed

Michael Porter, Competitive Advantage (1985). The usual view, then and now, is that competitive advantage belongs to firms: some are well run and some aren't. Porter's view went further than that: a firm is a collection of separate activities, and it wins or loses in each one on its own.

He grouped the activities into nine generic categories. Click any box to see what it is. Then turn on the Little Caesars overlay.

Part 2 · Two ideas people usually miss

These two matter more than the nine categories

Idea 1

The boxes are a starting point, not the answer

The nine are generic on purpose. The analysis only gets useful once you split an activity whose parts have different economics.

  • Airline "operations" covers gate turnaround and engine overhaul, which have almost nothing in common.
  • Little Caesars "marketing & sales" splits into national advertising (a fixed cost paid from a franchise fund) and the menu-board price, which isn't a cost at all. It's a promise.
TestIf every activity in your chain has the same cost driver, you haven't split them finely enough.
Idea 2

Advantage can sit between the boxes: linkages

A linkage exists when the cost or effectiveness of one activity depends on how another one is done. It's the least-used part of the framework.

Why linkages are hard to copy: a rival can see most single activities (store layouts, shelf prices, job postings, patents). What it can't easily see is which activity is subsidizing which.

Part 4 · Case

Little Caesars: a low cost position built one activity at a time

Its cost advantage isn't concentrated in one place. It's spread across the chain in small, deliberate decisions that reinforce each other.

The real savings come from what it doesn't do at allNo taking orders before making pizza. No drivers. At the Portal, no staffed counter. Removing an activity is the most reliable cost cut available, because the other option, doing the activity more cheaply than a rival, invites the rival to respond.

The linkage triangle: why "just sell a cheaper pizza" doesn't work

Each one only works because of the others:

  • Made-ahead production needs a narrow menu. Forecasting four products is manageable. Forecasting forty isn't.
  • A narrow menu needs a low price. Buyers demand variety when the price is high.
  • A low price needs high throughput per labor hour, which is exactly what made-ahead production delivers.

Pull out any one and the other two stop making sense. That's a chain in Porter's sense.

The price advantage has narrowed

2004: $5 Hot-N-Ready. One number: no coupon, no wait, no decision. It held for 18 years.
Jan 2022: rises to $5.55 (with 33% more pepperoni). No national price announced since.
Now: third-party menu trackers put it near $6.79. Meanwhile Domino's keeps running a "Best Deal Ever": any pizza, any toppings, $9.99.
July 2025: Little Caesars launches its first-ever value menu at $9.99, online only. Its CMO said the company sets trends rather than following them, but it was following. Online-only also moves the offer away from the walk-in, no-wait mechanic the whole cost chain was built to serve.
Still impressive, just a different storyLittle Caesars really did remove drivers, order-taking, counter labor, menu complexity and square footage. Net US units grew by 93 in 2025 while the category shrank (limited-service pizza −0.3%, Little Caesars +1.4%). But a large share of the savings was captured by the franchisor through the required supplier and the highest fee load in the category, not passed fully to operators or customers.
The lesson to rememberA value chain analysis tells you where the cost went out. It doesn't tell you who kept it. Ask both questions.
Part 6 · The opposite strategy

Own one link

Little Caesars does nearly the whole chain cheaply. The alternative is to take one activity and do it so far beyond everyone else that the rest of the industry has to come to you. Pick a firm.

Part 7 · Where the money sits

Profit pools

If activities have different economics, then somewhere along every chain there's a place where the money collects.

The U-Haul story (late 1990s)

U-Haul rented trucks for less than anyone else. It earned a 10% operating margin while the consumer truck-rental industry earned under 3%. But the trucks weren't where the money was.

The money was in boxes, tape, insurance, hitches and storage, which customers buy after signing the rental agreement, once they've stopped comparison shopping.

Where the idea comes from

Orit Gadiesh & James Gilbert, two Harvard Business Review articles (1998). An industry's profit pool is the total profit earned at every point along its value chain. The key claim: where profit concentrates in an industry is often very different from where revenue concentrates.

How to map one (Gadiesh & Gilbert's four steps)

1 Define the pool's boundaries. Where does the chain start and end? This step determines the answer, and no one has offered a principle for drawing the line.
2 Estimate the pool's total size. Total profit, not revenue, across every activity inside the boundary.
3 Estimate each activity's share. The hard step: activity-level margins are almost never disclosed. You rebuild them from comparable single-business companies and by triangulating.
4 Check and reconcile. The reading's dry point: a method that spends a quarter of its steps on reconciliation was written by people who knew the numbers wouldn't tie out.

Back to the warming rack

The $5 pizza was never about cheap cheese. It came from a chain with no drivers, no order-takers, no counter transaction, a required distributor, four products instead of forty, 1,400 square feet instead of 3,000, and a forecast instead of an order ticket. Each of those is an activity someone else performs and Little Caesars doesn't.

A chain also changes over time. The price is no longer $5. The value menu now matches a competitor's price instead of setting it. The franchisor kept much of the savings. The rival that once had the expensive chain rebuilt it around carryout. None of that showed up in the diagram. It showed up in the numbers underneath it.

The closing questionDraw your own chain, not the generic nine, split until each box has its own economics. Put a real cost and a real asset against each one. Then ask: if you could defend only one of these activities to the last dollar, which would it be, and is it the one you're funding?

Cheat sheet

Everything on one screen

Core idea

  • A firm has no costs. Activities do.
  • 9 generic activities: 5 primary (inbound, operations, outbound, marketing & sales, service) and 4 support (infrastructure, HR, technology development, procurement).
  • Margin = buyer's willingness to pay − total activity cost.

Two ideas people miss

  • Split until the parts have different economics.
  • Linkages: one activity's cost depends on another's. They're hard to copy because they can't be observed.
  • Amazon's regions: no single activity got cheaper.

5-step analysis

  • 1 Split up activities
  • 2 Cost + assets for each box
  • 3 Cost drivers (10 of them)
  • 4 Work the linkages, including suppliers and channels
  • 5 Own / buy / ride on someone else's brand

10 cost drivers

  • Scale · learning · capacity utilization · linkages · interrelationships across business units · vertical integration · timing of entry · discretionary policy choices · location · institutional factors

Little Caesars

  • Removing activities beats doing them cheaper.
  • Made-ahead ↔ narrow menu ↔ low price.
  • But: AUV ~69% of Domino's, 11% fees vs 9.5%, investment floor 4×, ACSI last.
  • Ask where the cost went out and who kept it.

Own one link

  • Inbound: ASML (only EUV maker, Zeiss stake)
  • Operations: TSMC (promise not to compete with customers), YKK (leased machines, brand on the customer's product)
  • Outbound: Amazon (cost center → sold as a product)
  • Service: Ritz-Carlton ($2,000 discretion per employee)

Profit pools

  • Gadiesh & Gilbert 1998: profit ≠ revenue concentration.
  • Value chain = efficiency. Profit pool = positioning.
  • Chart: x = revenue share, y = margin, area = profit.
  • 4 steps: boundaries, size, shares, reconcile.

Pool examples

  • U-Haul: cheap trucks, profitable boxes
  • Coffee: 21% to farming, 23% tax (Germany)
  • AutoNation: 22.8% of revenue → 77.2% of gross profit
  • Delta Amex = 141% of op. income; Apple 85% of profit; Booking > 2× Marriott
