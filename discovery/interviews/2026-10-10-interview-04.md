## What they actually do today

**The assignment loop — individual tax class, tax research memo (Q1).** The professor signed it
maybe two weeks out, mentioned it in class, and it was up on Canvas with a rubric; they did not open
it until about four days before it was due. Then: research through the tax code and a couple of
court cases — "the slow part" — outline in IRAC, draft over about two sessions in Google Docs, check
back against the rubric to make sure every piece was hit, one re-read for typos, Grammarly, submit on
Canvas about three hours before the deadline. What they second-guess is the citations: "That's the
part I always second-guess."

**The rubric-check loop (Q2), in their words:**

> "First thing I usually do is re-read the rubric line by line, and kind of self-grade. Like, did I
> actually hit this bullet, or am I fooling myself? That only gets me so far, though."

> "For this one, yeah, I pasted chunks into ChatGPT. Not to write it for me, more like, here's the
> rubric, here's my analysis section, am I missing anything?"

> "It's decent at catching when I didn't explain my reasoning enough. But I don't fully trust it on
> the actual tax law. It'll say something confidently that's just wrong. So I don't lean on it for
> the substance."

> "The other thing I do is text a buddy from my cohort. We're in the same classes, so I'll ask, 'Hey,
> did you get that it was deductible or not?' Just to sanity-check I'm not way off. That's usually
> more reassuring than the AI, honestly. Because he's working the same problem."

> "What I don't really do is office hours. I know I should, but by the time I'm actually working on
> it, it's the night before and the prof is long gone."

**Integrity (Q3) is a stated rule, not a fear:**

> "The rules are kind of a gray zone, you know? Some profs flat-out say no AI, some say it's fine for
> brainstorming, and a lot of them just don't say anything. Which, honestly, is the most stressful,
> because you're guessing."

> "My rule of thumb is: I'll use it to check my own work. Or help me outline. But I won't let it
> write sentences that end up in what I turn in. That way, if anyone ever asked, I could say the
> thinking and the words are mine. The research, the analysis."

> "I've definitely had that moment of hovering over submit thinking: could this get flagged by a
> Turnitin AI detector? And those detectors aren't even reliable. That's the scary part. I've heard
> of people getting false positives on stuff they actually wrote. So sometimes I'll keep my Google
> Docs version history, just in case I ever need to prove I actually wrote it myself."

The tax-memo professor "didn't say anything either way," so they fell back on their own rule and
"felt safe enough."

**Platform (Q4):** the question arrived as an assumption and was corrected — not BYU, Learning Suite
does not apply. What survives is the part the ICP actually depends on: **their courses are on
Canvas.** LMS coverage was not measured on this interview (caveat 1).

**Willingness to pay (closing question):** two conditions and a price band, both stated without
probing.

> "First, it'd have to save me real-time or real stress. Not just be 'a nice-to-have.' The two
> painful parts for me are the research slog and the 'am I actually hitting the rubric' anxiety. If
> it nailed those, I'm interested."

> "It has to be something I can use without feeling like I'm cheating. If it writes my memo for me,
> I won't touch it. That's a risk to my degree. But if it's more like a coach that checks my work
> against the rubric and says, 'You're weak here, you didn't cite this,' that I'd pay for."

> "Somewhere around 5 to 10 bucks a month is the impulse zone. 15 I'd think hard about. Anything
> subscriptiony over 20, and I'd probably just grind it out myself or lean on free ChatGPT. Unless
> it's CPA."

## Strongest signal

**The rubric-paste loop, confirmed by a second independent student, unprompted.** "Here's the
rubric, here's my analysis section, am I missing anything?" is the loop Homeroom exists to automate,
described in a course I have no connection to, by someone I had never interviewed. It is now the
most repeated behavior in the folder.

The stronger signal is a **constraint, not a feature**: the line they will not cross is sentences.
"If it writes my memo for me, I won't touch it. That's a risk to my degree." The thing they say they
would pay for is a rubric-scored critique of their own draft — which is the side the faculty
evidence already puts on the safe ground (45% of faculty call draft-plus-rubric feedback legitimate;
11% say that about AI writing the first draft).

Two counterweights, both new. (1) Their number-one pain is the *research* slog — tax code, court
cases, citations — which Homeroom does not touch at all; the rubric loop is their number two. (2)
Their tiebreaker when they are unsure is a classmate working the same problem, "more reassuring than
the AI." A tool that scores their draft has to beat that comparison on trust, not just on speed.

Also new to the notes: they keep Google Docs version history as **evidence of authorship**. Nobody
asked for a compliance feature; that is a student buying insurance against an accusation, which is
the same fear the ICP lists as a pain but here it is already costing them manual work.
