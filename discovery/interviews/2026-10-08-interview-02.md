## What I asked / what happened

Ran the behavior questions against a high-schooler instead of a college student. That one fact
reorganizes the whole interview: every surface (paper handouts, a turn-in tray, daily Canvas bell
ringers, a school-managed Grammarly seat) is the K-12 version of the same loop, and the school's own
IT policy — not any product decision — is what keeps AI off it.

Answers were concrete throughout: one named assignment, one named class, one named workaround, $0
spend. The two thin spots are the same ones as interview 01 — the Canvas-coverage question slid into
"have you tried to connect AI," and the closing question was hypothetical by design (see caveats).

## What they actually do today

Two loops, and they are different loops:

**Paper loop — Government, current-events project.** Handout given out "at the beginning of the
term," handwritten, turned "into his dashboard in his classroom." Workflow: find a news article
online → write about it → read it aloud at the start of class *or not* → submit on paper. No digital
artifact at any point, so nothing for a tool to touch.

**Canvas loop — Animal Science, bell ringers and exit tickets.** Daily, found on Canvas, no advance
notice: "It's on Canvas. Every day." The prompt is either a look-it-up question about the day's topic
or a recall question from a previous lesson; they type the answer "in a box, and then turn it in."

Rubric uncertainty (Q2), verbatim:

> "if I don't know—if I'm wondering what to do, then I'll just ask the teacher. But if I'm, like,
> wondering, like, if it's, like, good, then I'll probably just use AI."

Integrity pressure (Q3) is the *reverse* of interview 01's shape. There the worry was a blurred
policy; here the assumption is that detection is the binding constraint:

> "they all use, like, AI checkers, so you can't, like, really."
> "You still have to type it. So you might as well just use your own brain."

The workaround-for-AI is the secondhand one, and it is the most interesting thing in the interview:

> "if I had a Macbook, I would. But, like, my friends do it, because they, like, connect their AI to
> Canvas. So, like, their Bell Ringers and exit tickets, it goes on to their Bell Ringers, and then it
> types the answer and submits it for them."

Canvas coverage (Q4): **two of five classes**, and the exclusions are deliberate on the school's side
— "Math is just paper. Everything's on paper, so you can't cheat," and Government "everything's on
paper, just because he doesn't like Canvas." Never tried to get a token themselves; "the school
blocks all the AIs and stuff." **Learning Suite never came up at all.**

Spend (Q5): **$0.** Free ChatGPT, Grammarly via the school account ("I never have to go out and pay").
Math is "not really that hard for me," so no math tool.

WTP (Q6), the only forward-looking answer, and it is a *format* complaint, not a feature request:
show the work visually. They want a generated graph with the points plotted, how the points were
derived, and how they connect — "just like a teacher would" — plus clickable images or a YouTube
video, because

> "ChatGPT just, like, explains it to you like you already know. But if you don't know, then it
> doesn't help you at all."

## Strongest signal

**The rejection reason for AI turned out to be mechanical, not principled, and the kids who beat it
beat it by automating submission — not by writing better.** Ask the same question in two directions
and the two answers don't reconcile: "you still have to type it, so you might as well use your own
brain" (AI isn't worth it) versus the friends who "just don't have to do anything" (AI is worth it,
via Canvas ops). The differentiator between those two students is a MacBook and a Canvas hook, not
attitude or ethics.

Second signal, quieter and less convenient: the one thing they *would* pay for is an explanation that
teaches — work shown, visuals, a video — and they name ChatGPT's teaching as the failure. That lands
squarely on the PRD's tutor-first thesis, and it's the second interview in a row where a participant
volunteers "teach me like I don't know it" unprompted.

## Method caveats (script deviations — record as bets, not evidence)

1. **Off-ICP by construction.** Recruiting produced a high schooler. Everything about Canvas
   coverage, spend, and the disqualifier reads differently for a K-12 student on school-managed
   accounts, and the interview cannot be pooled with college-student interviews. The 20% rule was
   also not visibly respected — the interviewer restates questions and fills gaps often.
2. **Substitute workaround is secondhand.** The Canvas-to-AI automation is reported by P2 about
   "my friends," not done by P2. That is a secondhand claim in a conversation about a competitor —
   treat it as a lead to verify in an interview with one of those friends, not as observed behavior.
3. **Q4 slid again, same as interview 01.** "Are all of your classes on Canvas" became "have you ever
   tried to connect AI to Canvas." Coverage got answered by luck (two of five classes, with reasons);
   the token question never got asked cleanly. This is now the second time — fix the script.
4. **Q6 is hypothetical by design.** "What would it have to do for you to be willing to pay" invites
   solution-picturing; the answer ("graphs, images, a video") is a **bet** per the script's own note,
   not validation. No number appeared, and no existing spend was benchmarked against it.
